    ## Chapter Four: Ashfall in the Morning
    
    Mira's appraisal booth opened at sixth bell, which in Ashfall Colony's internal time-keeping meant the moment when the Upper Ring's automated lighting shifted from night-cycle amber to day-cycle white and the Scratch's inhabitants began the particular kind of movement that wasn't morning optimism so much as the resumption of survival.
    
    Ren was already waiting when Mira arrived. He was sitting on the steps outside her shuttered booth with his pack between his feet and his System interface minimized in the corner of his vision, running quiet. He looked like he always looked to anyone who didn't know him — unbothered, unhurried, faintly tired in the baseline way that Scratch residents were always faintly tired. Like a man who had just done his morning salvage run and was waiting for the market to open.
    
    Mira was a D-rank awakened, thirty-four years old, with a Status Appraisal ability she'd been running at Ashfall's appraisal booth for nine years. She'd awakened at eighteen and had promptly discovered that D-rank in the Scratch was a destination, not a launchpad. The guilds that recruited from Ashfall wanted C-rank minimum. The Sovereign Blades didn't recruit from the outer rim at all. Status Appraisal was a support ability — valuable in guild structures, essentially useless solo. She'd built a business out of it in the only market that had room for her.
    
    She was sharp. She'd been watching Ren since he was twelve and had started coming to her with questions about ability classifications that she eventually figured out he wasn't asking out of idle curiosity. He was building a functional knowledge base about awakened society from the ground up, using her as a resource, paying in information about Bone Fields structural changes she found genuinely useful. She'd decided she respected this and had started giving him better answers than she'd give a random walk-in.
    
    She looked at him now — the too-still quality of how he was sitting, the specific alert calm he used when he needed to appear un-alert — and said: "What happened."
    
    Not a question. Mira did not waste question marks.
    
    "I need you to run an appraisal," Ren said.
    
    She unlocked the booth, settled into her chair, activated her System interface. "Hold out your hand."
    
    He did. Her ability activated — he could see the slight shift in her focus that meant she was reading, the Status Appraisal layering his data over her vision. She was quiet for four seconds. Then five. Then her interface flickered and she made a sound he'd never heard from her before — a short, sharp exhale that wasn't quite a laugh.
    
    "It crashed," she said.
    
    "The appraisal?"
    
    "My interface crashed. It's never crashed." She restarted it, waited for it to stabilize, ran the appraisal again. This time her interface didn't crash — it simply returned a result that made her go very still in the specific way of someone receiving information they didn't know how to process.
    
    She turned the result toward him, projecting it so he could see what she was seeing:
    
    ```
    [STATUS APPRAISAL RESULT]
    Subject: Voss, R.
    Rank: [UNDEFINED — CLASSIFICATION PENDING]
    Active Abilities: [UNDEFINED — CLASSIFICATION PENDING]
    Health: 340 / 340
    Mana: [OVERFLOW — VALUE EXCEEDS APPRAISAL DISPLAY RANGE]
    Special Notes: ██████
    
    STATUS APPRAISAL SYSTEM NOTE:
    This result has been flagged as anomalous.
    Your Status Appraisal ability has been logged as accessing a restricted subject profile.
    This log will be transmitted to Iron Covenant Assessment Bureau within 48 hours.
    ```
    
    They both looked at it.
    
    Mira said: "That last part. About the log."
    
    "I saw it."
    
    "That means in forty-eight hours the Assessment Bureau is going to know I ran an appraisal on you."
    
    "Yes."
    
    "And they're going to want to know what I found."
    
    "Yes."
    
    Mira was quiet for a moment. She was running, Ren knew, the same kind of calculation he'd been running since he walked out of the Bone Fields — what she knew, what she didn't know, what the range of consequences looked like, what she could and couldn't control.
    
    She said: "I have a thing."
    
    She reached under her counter and produced a small data-chip. It was older than the one on his cord — the casing was scuffed, the indicator light slightly yellowed. She pressed it into his hand.
    
    "Your grandmother left that with me six months ago. She said: 'Give it to Ren when the System stops making sense.' " Mira's voice was carefully neutral in the way of someone who had decided not to feel things about something until she had more information. "I think the System stopping making sense is a reasonable description of what just happened to my interface."
    
    He looked at the chip. He already had one — the one Sera Voss had left him directly, the one he'd unlocked in Chamber 7-G. This was a second one.
    
    He pressed his thumb to it.
    
    It opened.
    
    No voice this time — just data. A single file, encrypted with a mana signature that matched his own. Inside: coordinates. Not planetary coordinates — galactic. A location in Sector 9's outer edge, formatted with precision that suggested whoever had recorded them knew exactly what they were pointing to.
    
    And a note, written in his grandmother's careful hand, that said:
    
    *When you need to run — and you will need to run — go here first. Someone is waiting. Has been waiting three years. She will know you by sight.*
    
    He closed the chip. He looked at Mira.
    
    "What did she tell you?" he asked. "When she left this."
    
    Mira was quiet for a moment. "She said you were going to have a very bad week and a very important life. She said you'd probably try to figure everything out by yourself first and that I should tell you — " Mira stopped. Started again. "She said: 'Tell him the accounting only works if you account for the people who already chose to be in your corner. He forgets to include them.'"
    
    Ren didn't say anything for a while.
    
    Then: "How much for your silence on the appraisal. Before the forty-eight hours are up."
    
    Mira looked at him steadily. "You can't pay me enough to lie to the Assessment Bureau, Ren. Even if you had the credits." She paused. "But I can tell them the appraisal system crashed and I never got a clean result. Which is technically true — it did crash, the first time. The second attempt produced an anomalous result that fell outside normal parameters. I can submit the crash report and omit the second attempt."
    
    He looked at her.
    
    "That's obstruction of the Assessment Bureau's logging protocol," he said.
    
    "Is it." She reached over and shut down her appraisal system entirely. "I'm having technical difficulties this morning. Very unfortunate. I'll need to submit a maintenance request." She met his eyes. "You have forty-eight hours before the crash log auto-transmits. After that I can't help you."
    
    He stood. He shouldered his pack.
    
    "Thank you," he said.
    
    Mira: "When you figure out what you are — " she stopped, shook her head. "Never mind. Go. I have a maintenance request to falsify."
    
    He went.
    
    ---
    
    He had forty-eight hours and fourteen to seventy-two on the other end, which meant he was working with a window of approximately fourteen hours before the first of those windows closed. He spent the first two hours of it doing something that would have looked, to anyone watching, like a man walking the Scratch's market district in a very inefficient pattern for no particular reason.
    
    He was mapping the watchers.
    
    The Scratch's market district ran along the base of the Upper Ring's retaining wall — a dense corridor of stalls and permanent shops and the semi-permanent operations that existed in the legally grey space between those two categories. It was busy by mid-morning, loud, packed with the kind of foot traffic that made individual surveillance difficult. But difficult wasn't impossible, and Ren had been studying the pattern of foot traffic and watcher placement in this district since he was thirteen.
    
    He identified three by the time he'd completed his first circuit. Two were Syndicate — their watcher rotation was familiar, he'd memorized it years ago as a matter of operational necessity. The third was not Syndicate. He'd never seen the third before: a woman, late twenties, C-rank awakened by the mana signature his newly functional senses could now read like text. She was good. She held her cover — a produce stall customer, lingering — with professional competence.
    
    She wasn't watching the market. She was watching him.
    
    He bought a meal from a stall he regularly bought meals from, sat at a familiar location, ate without any visible sign of having noticed her, and thought about what her presence meant.
    
    Someone had moved faster than fourteen hours. Someone had already had assets in Ashfall Colony before the gate opened — before he'd been flagged. This was not a reactive response to his awakening. This was a pre-positioned surveillance operation.
    
    Someone had known, before today, that Ashfall Colony was worth watching.
    
    He finished eating. He stood up. He walked, without urgency, toward the residential blocks — and then, three turns into a route that he knew from a decade of navigation and she did not, he was gone.
    
    He found a maintenance access point in the Scratch's waste-reclamation infrastructure, a route that ran below the residential district, and followed it to the eastern edge of the colony. He came up into a utility corridor near the colony's vehicle bay, found a corner behind a power relay that wasn't visible from either entrance, and waited.
    
    Twenty minutes later, his message alert chimed.
    
    Cai: *On my way. ETA six hours. Also I just died.*
    
    Ren: *How.*
    
    Cai: *Sector 8 B-rank. Long story. Currently in the reset window. Have replanned approach. Will tell you about it. Why, what did you do.*
    
    Ren: *I found a gate in the Bone Fields. I went in.*
    
    A pause. Then: *You went in. To an unregistered gate. Alone. As an unawakened F-rank.*
    
    Ren: *I'm not unawakened anymore.*
    
    A longer pause. Then: *I'm going to need you to explain this very carefully and also I'm bringing the good coffee because this sounds like a multiple-cups conversation.*
    
    Ren: *There's a C-rank watcher in the market district who wasn't there yesterday. Pre-positioned. Come in through the vehicle bay, not the main gate.*
    
    Cai: *...You've been awake for what, four hours? How are you already being surveilled?*
    
    Ren: *It's a long story.*
    
    Cai: *...I'm bringing a lot of coffee.*
    
    Ren put his interface to minimum display and settled in to wait, running the accounting in the quiet, methodical way his grandmother had taught him. The surveillance operation — too fast, too organized, suggesting foreknowledge. The appraisal crash — Mira's forty-eight hour window. The coordinates on the second chip. The note: *someone is waiting, has been waiting three years.*
    
    His grandmother had placed an asset at those coordinates three years before her death. Someone who knew to expect him. Who knew him by sight, which meant they had his image, which meant the surveillance on him predated this morning by at least three years.
    
    He was being watched. Had been watched. While Sera Voss was alive and actively covering his existence.
    
    Which meant she'd known there were watchers. Which meant she'd been running a counter-surveillance operation of her own for years, from a Scratch bunkhouse, with no abilities and no resources and nothing but the specific competence of a woman who had understood the stakes perfectly and had refused to let them win.
    
    He sat in the utility corridor behind a power relay and added his grandmother to his mental accounting of assets.
    
    She'd been playing this longer than he'd known. She'd been playing it better than he'd known. She'd left him three separate information caches — the spoken message on the first chip, the coordinates and note on the second chip, the human asset waiting at the coordinates — and she'd positioned all three with enough care that they only became accessible in the right sequence.
    
    He thought: *you were extraordinary, Sera Voss.*
    
    Then he let himself feel it for exactly sixty seconds — the grief he'd been filing away, the specific hollow that had been in his chest for six months and was now, somehow, both larger and more navigable than it had been. She'd known what was coming. She'd spent six months dying and using every hour she had left to prepare him for today.
    
    Sixty seconds.
    
    Then he put it away and returned to the accounting.

    ## Chapter Five: Cai Draven Arrives With Bad Timing
    
    Cai Draven came through the vehicle bay six hours later with a pack that was objectively too large for any reasonable salvage operation, a thermal container of actual coffee — not the synthetic compound that passed for coffee in the Scratch market but actual coffee beans from somewhere that grew them — and an energy about him that could charitably be described as inappropriately cheerful.
    
    He was twenty-five years old, built like someone who'd spent their formative years doing things that required surviving physical consequences, with dark skin and close-cut hair and a scar running from his left jawline to below the collar of his jacket that he'd gotten on his forty-first death and had never bothered to explain to anyone's satisfaction. His System interface currently showed S-rank, which was an anomaly in Ashfall Colony the way a star would be an anomaly in a room.
    
    He'd been out here running Sector 8 dungeons for three months not because the credits were good — they weren't, not for an S-rank, who could be running A-rank dungeons in the core sectors for twenty times the payout — but because Ren was here and Cai had decided, two years ago during a scavenging run that had gone wrong in a way that involved a structural collapse and the specific calculus of who knew about the trap and who didn't, that Ren Voss was the most interesting person he'd met in two hundred and thirteen lives.
    
    He set the thermal container down in front of Ren, looked him over with the assessing attention that he usually hid under the chaotic surface energy, and said: "You look the same."
    
    "The awakening was eleven seconds," Ren said. "I wasn't expecting visible changes."
    
    "Your mana signature is not the same." Cai settled across from him in the utility corridor, reaching for the thermal container with the ease of someone for whom eating and drinking in utility corridors was a familiar operational setting. "I can feel it from here. It's — " He frowned slightly. "It doesn't taste right."
    
    Ren: "How does a mana signature taste."
    
    "S-rank, remember? Enhanced sensory processing." Cai poured coffee, passed a cup to Ren, poured another for himself. "Every awakened being has a mana signature. Once you've met enough of them, you start developing intuitions about what different ability types feel like. Combat types have a specific flavor, support types have another, the Irregular classifications have a whole range." He looked at Ren steadily. "Yours doesn't have a flavor."
    
    "What does that mean."
    
    "It means your mana is present — a lot of it, more than I've ever felt in something that isn't an SS-rank or above — but it doesn't register as *anything* in my sensory categorization. It's like trying to taste water. The water is there, you can feel it, but it doesn't have a taste because taste requires something to interact with and water is..." He trailed off. "That was a better analogy in my head."
    
    "You're saying my ability exists outside the categories your sensory processing uses to identify abilities."
    
    "I'm saying your ability doesn't fit in any slot my experience has built for ability identification. Which, for an S-rank with two hundred and — " He stopped.
    
    Ren: "Two hundred and thirteen deaths."
    
    Cai went very still for a fraction of a second. Then: "I was going to say two hundred and something."
    
    "You died this morning in Sector 8. That was two-thirteen. You told me."
    
    "I told you in the reset window, which technically — "
    
    "Cai."
    
    A pause. Cai looked at his coffee cup. He had a particular expression that appeared sometimes in quiet moments — not the energy he wore like armor, but the thing underneath it. Older. More tired. The quality of someone who has processed a very large number of experiences and is finding the weight of them more present than usual.
    
    He said: "Two hundred and thirteen. Yes."
    
    Ren said: "What do you lose at two hundred and thirteen?"
    
    The pause was longer this time.
    
    "Warmth," Cai said. "In my hands. The thermal sensation — I can still feel texture, pressure, physical contact. Just not temperature differentiation through the hands anymore." He turned his cup in his fingers. "The coffee is hot. I know it's hot because the steam is visible and because I know what coffee does when it's fresh. I can't feel it."
    
    Ren looked at him. Cai met his eyes with the particular steadiness of someone who was used to being looked at and had decided not to flinch from it.
    
    Ren said: "At what point did it start."
    
    Cai: "Taste at death one-eighty-seven. Smell at two-oh-one. Warmth at two-thirteen." He paused. "There's a pattern. I've been tracking it. The intervals are shortening."
    
    Ren: "How many more."
    
    Cai: "I don't know. The degradation isn't linear — each loss is taking something slightly more fundamental than the last. If the pattern holds..." He shrugged. The shrug had too much weight behind it for the casual gesture it was supposed to be. "A while yet. Probably."
    
    "And you didn't tell me."
    
    "It's my death count. I can manage my own death count."
    
    "Cai."
    
    "Ren."
    
    They looked at each other for a moment. Then Cai's expression shifted back toward the familiar — not entirely, not the way it would have before this conversation, but enough. "Are we going to have a feeling about this right now, or are you going to tell me about the gate and the surveillance operation and whatever is making your mana signature taste like a concept instead of an ability."
    
    Ren told him.
    
    All of it — the gate, the constructs, the crystal, the awakening, the System notifications, the Shadow at the edge of the chamber. His grandmother's two chips. The C-rank watcher in the market. Mira's forty-eight hour window. The coordinates.
    
    Cai listened without interrupting, which was unusual — Cai's default processing style involved verbal interjection. When Ren finished, Cai was quiet for long enough that the silence started to feel intentional.
    
    Then he said: "Your grandfather hacked the System from the inside, rewrote core directives, left a message in the deep code, and got erased from reality. And the thing he left you is a single shadow in the corner of an impossible dungeon that doesn't speak."
    
    "Yes."
    
    "And your awakened ability is undefined. The System literally cannot categorize it."
    
    "Yes."
    
    "And there are currently fourteen entities at Architect-clearance level who know you exist, two of whom are considering killing you."
    
    "Yes."
    
    Cai picked up his coffee cup. He looked at it for a moment, as if confirming, again, that it was hot. Then he set it down and looked at Ren with the specific expression that Ren had come to recognize over two years as Cai's real face — not the energy, not the grin, but the thing underneath: the weariness and the attention and the quality of someone who had lived enough versions of enough situations to know what the important variables were.
    
    He said: "Okay. I'm staying."
    
    Ren: "You were going to go back to Sector 8."
    
    Cai: "Sector 8 will still have dungeons in it without me. The S-rank guild circuit will survive my absence." He picked up his pack, which was objectively too large, and patted it with the air of someone who had prepared for contingencies. "I packed for extended operation. Didn't know that's what I was packing for, but I've learned to pack for extended operation as a general principle."
    
    Ren: "How extended."
    
    Cai thought about this for a moment.
    
    "Your grandmother," he said, "spent nineteen years running a counter-surveillance operation from a Scratch bunkhouse to keep you alive. The System couldn't classify your ability. An AI that's been running for four billion years flagged your birth before you were born." He met Ren's eyes. "I have died two hundred and thirteen times, Ren. I know how to recognize something that matters. This is the thing that matters." A pause. "Also you once warned me about a trap when you could have let me walk into it and taken my pack, which on the outer rim is statistically very unusual behavior, and I've been paying that debt ever since."
    
    The debt comment was deflection — Ren knew it, Cai knew he knew it, the information got exchanged anyway. This was how they communicated about things that mattered: through the data that sat adjacent to the thing, rather than directly at it.
    
    Ren said: "There's a watcher in the market."
    
    Cai: "C-rank. I clocked her on the way in." He grinned, and this one was real — the particular grin that appeared when he'd already thought several moves ahead and found the situation more manageable than the initial data suggested. "She's pre-positioned, which means her handler expected your awakening. Which means they had a timeline. Which means there's an information source." He cracked his knuckles. "I know most of the information networks in Sector 9. Give me twelve hours and I'll tell you exactly whose she is."
    
    Ren: "We have fourteen hours maximum before the first window closes."
    
    Cai: "Twelve hours, then." He stood, re-shouldered the too-large pack. "Where are we operating from."
    
    Ren thought for a moment. The Scratch bunkhouse was compromised — if they had pre-positioned surveillance, they had his known residence. Mira's booth was blown. The Bone Fields were potentially monitored.
    
    He thought about the vehicle bay they were currently sitting adjacent to, and about the coordinates on his grandmother's second chip, and about the note that said *go here first.*
    
    He said: "We're leaving Ashfall."
    
    Cai raised an eyebrow.
    
    Ren: "My grandmother left coordinates. Someone's waiting there. And whatever's coming — " He glanced at his System interface, at the pending classification, at the cascade of notifications that were sitting in his queue like a series of very politely worded threats. "We need to be mobile before the forty-eight hour window closes."
    
    Cai: "The coffee can travel."
    
    Ren: "Yes."
    
    "Good." Cai turned toward the vehicle bay. "I'll get us transport. Don't tell me how." He paused. "Do you have credits."
    
    Ren: "Forty, approximately."
    
    Cai: "I have considerably more. Also a favor owed by a D-rank who runs the vehicle bay's maintenance schedule. We'll be fine." He started moving, then stopped. "One question."
    
    Ren: "Yes."
    
    Cai: "The shadow in the dungeon. The one that didn't speak. Did it feel — I'm trying to think of the right word." He seemed genuinely uncertain, which was unusual. "Did it feel like something that was going to be a problem."
    
    Ren thought about it. About the quality of the shadow's silence — the attention in it, the patience, the way it had watched him prepare to leave without moving to stop him or follow him.
    
    "No," he said. "It felt like something that was going to be very useful and very difficult to explain."
    
    Cai was quiet for a moment.
    
    "Okay," he said. "That tracks."
    
    He went to arrange transport.

    ## Chapter Six: The Watcher's Name
    
    The vehicle bay operated on Ashfall Colony's informal economy the way most things in the Scratch operated — officially, it was an Iron Covenant municipal facility for registered transport craft. Unofficially, it was run by a rotating cast of D-rank and E-rank awakened who had found that the Covenant's outer-rim administration was too underfunded to notice what happened in the maintenance schedules as long as the bays were available when the Covenant needed them.
    
    Cai's favor — Ren didn't ask, Cai didn't explain — produced a mid-range personal transport with enough fuel for three sector transits and a manifest listing two registered D-rank awakened on a routine salvage survey. The manifest was, technically, a document. What it described bore a flexible relationship with reality.
    
    They loaded in forty minutes. Ren's contribution to the packing was one bag and a speed of preparation that Cai observed with the look of someone recognizing a familiar habit.
    
    "You've packed to run before," Cai said.
    
    "I've packed to run since I was thirteen." Ren checked the transport's sensor package — basic, outer-rim standard, but functional. "When I figured out the surveillance on my building. I never knew who was running it, but I knew it was there."
    
    "And you didn't leave."
    
    "I had no information about where to run to. Leaving without information is movement without strategy." He ran a final check of the transport's identification beacons — clean, nothing flagged. "Now I have coordinates."
    
    Cai settled into the co-pilot seat and produced a data-pad from somewhere in the too-large pack. He'd spent ten of their twelve hours working a channel through his network — a series of contacts built over two years of Sector 9 operation, the kind of social infrastructure that happened when you were a friendly, chaotic, apparently harmless S-rank who happened to be very interested in knowing things.
    
    "The watcher," he said, pulling up a file. "C-rank awakened. Ability: Resonance Tracking — she can follow a specific mana signature through obstacles, walls, crowds. Useful for exactly one thing." He turned the data-pad toward Ren. "She's Iron Covenant Intelligence Bureau. Filed as an outer-rim monitoring asset. Asset code places her under the authority of — " He paused.
    
    Ren: "Whose authority."
    
    Cai: "Director-General Seris Vayne. Intelligence Bureau chief. Based on Earth-Prime." He set the pad down. "The most senior intelligence officer in the Iron Covenant."
    
    The name meant nothing to Ren. He filed it.
    
    "How long has she been in Ashfall," he said.
    
    Cai: "The asset? Three years. She arrived in Ashfall Colony three years ago and has been running a static monitoring operation at this location ever since."
    
    Three years. The same timeline as the coordinates on his grandmother's second chip. *Someone is waiting. Has been waiting three years.*
    
    He thought about this.
    
    Seris Vayne had placed a monitoring asset in Ashfall Colony three years ago — watching him, presumably. His grandmother had placed a counter-asset at the coordinates three years ago. Both, simultaneously, suggesting that something three years ago had made multiple parties decide that Ren Voss's location needed to be tracked.
    
    "What happened three years ago," he said.
    
    Cai: "In Sector 9? Nothing significant. Outer-rim sectors don't generate significant events as a rule." He paused. "In the broader galaxy — there was an Intelligence Bureau investigation into a series of gate anomalies in Sectors 7 through 11. Officially classified as geological mana activity. Investigation closed without public findings."
    
    Ren thought about a gate building pressure for two hundred years beneath forty feet of composite.
    
    "The Bone Fields gate," he said. "It was showing preliminary activation signals three years ago. Before it fully opened."
    
    Cai: "Which is when the Intelligence Bureau flagged it. Which is when Vayne positioned an asset here." He was quiet for a moment. "Your grandmother positioned her counter-asset at the same time."
    
    "She knew. She was watching the Bone Fields too. She knew the gate was approaching activation." Ren looked at the transport's navigation display. "She knew everything, Cai. She's been running this for longer than three years. She positioned assets, she prepared data caches, she made sure Mira had the second chip. She knew exactly what was coming and she spent the last years of her life making sure I'd be ready for it."
    
    Cai: "She sounds like she was extraordinary."
    
    "She was." Ren input the coordinates from the second chip into the navigation system. "She was a Scratch resident with no abilities and no resources and she outmaneuvered an Iron Covenant Intelligence Bureau operation for at least three years."
    
    He paused.
    
    He said: "I want to know what Seris Vayne knows. About my grandfather. About the gate. About why she's been running surveillance on an unranked F-rank in the outer rim for three years without moving." He brought the navigation system online. "The asset in the market district is the Bureau's only visible piece in Ashfall right now. The moment we leave, she'll report our departure."
    
    Cai: "So we're running toward information, not away from surveillance."
    
    "We're going to the coordinates. Whatever my grandmother left there — whoever she left there — that's our next data point." He checked the transport manifest one last time. "And then we're going to figure out exactly who Seris Vayne is before she decides what to do about us."
    
    Cai: "You think she hasn't decided yet."
    
    Ren: "She's had three years and a monitoring asset. If she was going to make a move, she would have made it before today." He looked at the Ashfall Colony skyline through the transport's viewport — the Upper Ring's lights, the Scratch's dark, the Bone Fields' silent ruins. "She's waiting. She's waited three years. She's waiting to see what I am."
    
    He thought about the System notification: *Two of those response protocols include immediate termination of subject.*
    
    Two out of fourteen. Twelve others. And at least one of the twelve had a monitoring operation that was three years old and had never moved to terminate.
    
    "She's not sure what I am," he said. "She's not sure if I'm a threat or an asset. And she's too careful to move until she is."
    
    Cai: "What do we do with that."
    
    Ren: "We give her information. Controlled. Curated." He set the transport in motion, guiding it toward the vehicle bay's exit route. "We make sure that when she decides, she's deciding based on what we've given her to decide with."
    
    Cai was quiet for a moment, watching Ashfall Colony recede through the viewport.
    
    "You know," he said, "most people who just had their first awakening spend the first week running combat drills."
    
    Ren: "Most people's first awakening wasn't staged by a dead man two hundred years ago to trigger a fourteen-entity alert cascade."
    
    "Fair." Cai settled back in his seat. "I'm going to eat something. Do you want anything."
    
    "No."
    
    "Okay." The sounds of Cai locating food in the too-large pack. Then, quietly, almost to himself: "She sounds like she would have liked knowing you figured it out."
    
    Ren: "My grandmother."
    
    "Yeah."
    
    Ren kept his eyes on the navigation display. The coordinates his grandmother had left him sat at the center of the screen, marking a destination in the outer edge of Sector 9 that the colony's public maps listed as unoccupied transit space.
    
    He said: "She knew. She always knew I would."
    
    The transport cleared Ashfall Colony's airspace. The Bone Fields shrank to a grey smudge below, and then to nothing, and then to the nothing that distance makes of everything.
    
    Somewhere in the colony, a C-rank monitoring asset activated a communication link and reported: *Subject departed. Unknown destination. Unknown companion. Requesting guidance.*
    
    Four hundred light-years away, in an office on Earth-Prime that was not officially listed in any directory, a woman named Seris Vayne received the report, read it once, and made no response for eleven minutes.
    
    Then she opened the file she had maintained for nineteen years. She looked at the most recent photograph — taken six days ago, a young man in the Bone Fields, running his salvage route in the pre-dawn dark, unbothered and unaware — and thought about three years of watching, and about the forty-eight hours she'd been given, and about the specific quality of patience required to wait for something you couldn't control.
    
    She had made a decision. Not the final one — not yet. But a preliminary one.
    
    She was going to see what he did next.
