    ## Chapter Six: Hunted
    
    The deep Bone Fields access route was not on any map because it had been built as a maintenance corridor during Meridian Station's original construction and then sealed when the station's lower levels were redesigned three years before the gate burst. It ran below the standard Bone Fields floor level, which meant below the crystallized mana residue deposits, below the secondary gate formations, below the collapsed structural layers that made most of the deep Bone Fields genuinely unnavigable.
    
    Here, it was just a corridor. Old, dust-heavy, its walls the original station composite from before the burst — unmarked by the event that had remade everything above it, preserved by the depth and the sealing. Ren's headlamp picked out details: maintenance access panels, still labeled in the pre-collapse station identification system, the kind of labeling that told you what function each section had served and what systems ran through the walls. He found himself reading them automatically. Cataloguing.
    
    Lyra moved ahead of him with a light of her own — a compact illuminator clipped to her collar, hands free. She navigated the corridor with the confidence of prior physical reconnaissance, her feet finding the correct path without hesitation at the branch points.
    
    "You've been down here," Ren said.
    
    "Three times. I ran the route twice before I was certain of the structural integrity."
    
    "The original construction records gave you the layout."
    
    "They gave me the design. Physical verification is separate." She took a left branch without slowing. "The design and the reality diverge in four locations where post-burst settling shifted the structural foundation. I mapped the divergence."
    
    Cai, behind Ren, made a quiet sound that combined admiration and mild alarm. "You prepared for this specifically."
    
    "I prepared for the event the probability chains indicated," Lyra said. "I didn't know exactly what form it would take. I knew it would require rapid concealed transit through the lower structure." She paused at a junction, consulted the mental map she was clearly running, and took the right branch. "Preparation for a class of outcomes is more efficient than preparation for specific scenarios."
    
    Ren filed this. Her operational methodology was interesting — the probability-sense informing preparation without constraining it to a single anticipated outcome. He was constructing a model of how she thought, the way he constructed models of everything that mattered.
    
    Above them, through the corridor's ceiling, through the Bone Fields' floor, through however many meters of collapsed station infrastructure separated them from the colony's working levels, the ranked unit was moving. He could feel them — six signatures, organized, moving in a search pattern that was methodical rather than urgent. They were confident. They had the mana trace from the gate activation and they expected it to lead them to him directly.
    
    They were not accounting for him being sixty meters below the level they were searching.
    
    "How long can we stay down here?" he asked.
    
    "The corridor connects to an exit shaft on the colony's outer structural wall," Lyra said. "The shaft opens to the exterior docking framework — the external scaffolding. From there we have access to the small-craft berths on the outer ring's lower edge."
    
    "Where no one pays attention to departing small craft," Cai said.
    
    "Where the departure logging is handled by automated systems with a forty-minute processing delay," Lyra confirmed. "Which means we can be off the colony before our departure registers anywhere."
    
    "You have a ship," Ren said.
    
    A brief pause. "I have access to a ship."
    
    "There's a difference."
    
    "There's a small difference. The ship is available and functional and the person who owns it owes me a professional courtesy." She glanced back at him. "The details are not relevant to the next thirty minutes."
    
    He let it go. The details would become relevant at some point. He'd return to them.
    
    The corridor ran for another four hundred meters before the exit shaft, and in those four hundred meters Ren did what he always did in transit: he thought. He ran the situation through his operational framework, the one he'd built over years of managing survival in a place where the margins were thin and the information was incomplete. He sorted what he knew from what he inferred, what he inferred from what he'd extrapolated, and where the gaps were.
    
    What he knew: a gate had opened, he had awakened, his interface was reading errors, a ranked unit had arrived at the colony within hours of the awakening.
    
    What he inferred: the gate had monitoring protocols that had signaled someone when it activated, the signal had been acted on immediately, the response time indicated the monitoring had been ongoing and the response team had been pre-positioned for rapid deployment.
    
    What he'd extrapolated: someone had been watching Kael Voss's gate for two hundred years, with resources sufficient to maintain monitoring and a rapid-response capability across that time span. That was not an individual. That was an institution. An institution with the patience and resources to maintain a two-century surveillance operation on a sealed gate in the outer rim.
    
    The Sovereignty was the only institution in the galaxy with those resources and that patience.
    
    Which meant the Sovereignty knew about Kael Voss's gate. Had known about it for two hundred years. Had been unable to open it — because the bloodline authentication meant that only a Voss descendant with the right genetic signature could get the constructs to step aside, and the Sovereignty had presumably tried every other method. Had been waiting for the gate to activate.
    
    And now it had. And he was the reason.
    
    He thought about what his grandmother had said: *don't let them find the gate. It didn't open for them.*
    
    The gate had re-sealed when he left. He'd watched it close. The hatch above Chamber 7-G's sub-basement had re-formed seamlessly. The Sovereignty's unit was searching above, at the Bone Fields' standard accessible levels, following a mana trace that had originated from a location they couldn't currently identify because the entry point had ceased to exist.
    
    That was a temporary advantage. Determined search efforts on a fixed location would eventually find the right sub-basement, and a sufficiently high-ranked individual with the right abilities could probably force access that a bloodline-locked mechanism would ordinarily prevent. He had time. Not unlimited time.
    
    He also had his mana-sense, which was still calibrating, and which was showing him something interesting about the six signatures above.
    
    They were B-rank. Standard military configuration — the mana distribution of trained combat operatives, the signature pattern of people who had developed their abilities through systematic Sovereignty military advancement rather than irregular dungeon running. Professional. Coordinated. Experienced.
    
    But not high B-rank. Not the top of the tier. And the Sovereignty, if it had maintained a two-hundred-year watch on this gate, would know that what was on the other side of it had been built by an S-rank entity of exceptional capability. If they sent people to collect whoever had activated the gate, why would they send mid-tier B-ranks?
    
    Either they were underestimating what had awakened. Or these were not the first response.
    
    These were the fast response — positioned nearby for speed. The actual response was coming separately, on a different timeline.
    
    He filed this. Priority information. Required updating the situational model.
    
    They reached the exit shaft.
    
    ---
    
    The shaft was a vertical tube, two meters diameter, its walls fitted with the original station maintenance ladder — steel composite, corroded at the joints but structurally intact where it counted. Lyra went first, climbing with the efficiency of someone who'd done it before and knew where to put her hands. Cai went second. Ren went last, pulling the access hatch closed below him — a precaution that might not matter and cost nothing to take.
    
    The climb was forty meters. At the top, the shaft opened into the colony's outer structural framework — the space between the habitat ring's interior wall and its exterior hull, a maintenance zone that ran the colony's circumference and accessed every external system. It was not designed for people to be in regularly, which meant it was not monitored regularly. Lyra had clearly verified this.
    
    She was already moving along the framework when he reached the top, navigating the structural elements — conduits, support struts, system housings — with the same pre-mapped confidence she'd used in the maintenance corridor below. He followed. The outer hull was three meters to his right, and through it — through the composite and the vacuum seal and the external scaffolding — he could feel the void of open space and the distant signature of the colony's exterior docking structures.
    
    The small-craft berths were on the outer ring's lower edge. Lyra brought them to an access point in the hull — a maintenance egress, a small airlock-style hatch designed for EVA access to the exterior. She ran a code on the hatch's internal panel. It opened.
    
    "The scaffolding outside connects to the small-craft berths," she said. "It's a two-minute transit along the exterior. Vacuum exposure — you'll need this." She produced two breathing masks from an inner pocket, passed one to Ren and one to Cai. Compact, rated for short EVA duration. She had one already clipped to her collar, he noted.
    
    "You carry emergency masks," Cai said.
    
    "I carry emergency equipment," she said. "Yes. Regularly."
    
    Cai looked at the mask, looked at Lyra, and put the mask on with the expression of someone revising their assessment of a person in a positive direction.
    
    The exterior was the void's particular quality of silence — not the absence of sound, which you could get in a sealed room, but the presence of a scale that made sound irrelevant. The scaffolding ran along the colony's outer hull, a lattice of structural metal and access lines, the colony's exterior systems arrayed along it: sensor clusters, communication arrays, docking guide mechanisms. Below — in the orientation that the colony's gravity definition called below — the scaffolding connected to the small-craft berth assembly, a series of enclosed bays accessed from the exterior.
    
    They moved along the scaffolding. Ren kept his mana-sense extended. The six ranked signatures were still above and inside, no longer moving in his direction — they had shifted their search pattern, which meant they had updated their tracking information and found it less reliable than they'd expected. That was the gate's resealing working in his favor. They knew the general location but not the specific one.
    
    And they couldn't feel him down here, sixty meters below their search altitude and outside the colony's hull. The void attenuated mana signatures — one of the properties that made space an effective barrier between dungeon ecosystems and the broader inhabited galaxy. He was, for the moment, invisible to anything they were using to track him.
    
    The small-craft berths were ahead. Bay seven, Lyra had said. Third from the left in the lower assembly.
    
    She'd been right about having access to a ship. Bay seven contained a vessel that was technically registered to a private logistics company operating out of the outer-rim transit network — a courier-class ship, small enough to thread through traffic without attracting attention, large enough to make interstellar transit without a support vessel. The berth's access panel responded to Lyra's authorization code.
    
    "Professional courtesy," Cai said, looking at the ship.
    
    "The owner and I have an arrangement," Lyra said. "She leaves ships in useful locations. I use them when useful. We settle accounts when paths cross."
    
    "That's either a very sophisticated partnership or a very relaxed definition of property rights."
    
    "Both," she said, and opened the boarding hatch.
    
    Ren stepped aboard. The ship was clean — maintained, functional, no sign of regular occupation. A working vessel kept ready. He ran his mana-sense across it: the drive system was charged, the life support was active in standby mode, the navigation system was running. Ready to go.
    
    He thought about Ashfall Colony above them — the bunkhouse where his seven housemates were probably waking up right now, the Scratch Market starting its morning operation, Mira at her appraisal booth. His scraper, left in Chamber 7-G. The forty-four credits of mana residue he'd already sold.
    
    He'd lived in Ashfall Colony his entire life.
    
    He thought about his grandmother's chip, now inert in his pocket. About Kael Voss and a gate sealed two hundred years ago and a surveillance operation that had been running since before his parents were born. About an S-rank entity who had chosen to be erased rather than captured, and left a gate for whoever his bloodline produced who was worth the wait.
    
    He thought about the approaching second response — the real one, the one the B-rank fast-response unit was buying time for. Whatever that response was, it was going to arrive in Ashfall Colony looking for him.
    
    He was not going to be here when it did.
    
    "Where does the arrangement take us?" he asked Lyra.
    
    "Wherever is useful," she said. She was already in the pilot's seat. "I have a destination in mind. You'll hear the rationale once we're in transit."
    
    "That's a short answer to an important question."
    
    "We have time for long answers once we're moving," she said. "Right now we have approximately six minutes before the automated departure logging system runs its cycle. I'd like to be gone before that happens."
    
    Cai was already strapping in. He looked at Ren with the expression of someone who found the situation interesting and the urgency appropriate.
    
    Ren sat down and strapped in.
    
    The ship's drive engaged. The berth access opened. And Ashfall Colony — his home, his graveyard, his only reference point for nineteen years — slid away behind them into the dark.
