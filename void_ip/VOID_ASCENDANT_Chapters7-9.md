# VOID ASCENDANT: THE ETERNAL HIERARCHY
    ## Book 1 — Chapters 7–9
    
    ---
    
    # CHAPTER 7: LACUNA
    
    The first jump took them fourteen light-years in eleven seconds.
    
    Ren had never jumped before. Unranked residents of Scrap Quarter stations didn't travel — travel required transit permits, transit permits required rank verification, rank verification required a chip that actually functioned in the System's architecture. He'd spent his entire life inside a structure roughly four kilometers end to end, and the concept of *fourteen light-years* had been, until this moment, an abstraction.
    
    The jump drive engaged with a sound like reality deciding to do something different. The viewport went from star-scattered black to absolute white to the specific quality of darkness that existed between systems — deeper than station-dark, with no reflected light from any nearby structure, just the ancient scattered photons of distant suns that had been traveling for millions of years to reach this particular patch of nothing.
    
    Ren gripped the armrest. Released it when he realized he was gripping it.
    
    "First jump?" Lyra asked. She was watching the navigation readout rather than him, which he appreciated.
    
    "That obvious?"
    
    "Everyone does the armrest thing." She ran a post-jump diagnostic with the efficiency of someone who'd done it ten thousand times. "We're clean. No tracking signatures, no pursuit vector, no Covenant transponder pings. The waiver Cai got us covered the station departure — as far as their logs are concerned, we filed a standard route to the Delmarth resupply ring and that's where we're headed."
    
    "And where are we actually headed?"
    
    She was quiet for a moment. The kind of quiet that meant she was doing something with the Probability Eyes — checking futures, running chains, looking for the version of this conversation that led somewhere useful rather than disastrous.
    
    "That depends on what's in the secondary crystal," she said finally.
    
    Ren had been thinking the same thing. He pulled both crystals from his inner pocket and held them in his palm — the primary authentication crystal and the secondary data storage Kael had held toward the recording's camera two centuries ago. They were warm. Not mechanically warm, not friction or body heat — warm in the way that things are warm when they've been waiting a long time for something to finally happen.
    
    He pressed the secondary crystal against the primary.
    
    The interface opened immediately.
    
    Not the ghost-blue of standard chip readouts. The gold again, flooding his vision, and then past the gold a cascade of information that arrived not as text but as structure — the way you might understand a building not by reading its blueprints but by somehow being inside it all at once. Fifty years of compiled research. The shape of the Architect's true history. The locations of eleven sites that Kael had designated as significant. The coordinates of a place simply labeled:
    
    **[THE FIRST DOOR]**
    
    And beneath that, a note in Kael's handwriting — or what the crystal rendered as handwriting, the intimacy of someone who knew they were leaving a message for one specific person:
    
    *This is where I ran out of time. This is where you start. I don't know what you'll find there — I never made it inside. But I know what the Architect is afraid of, and it's afraid of this place most of all.*
    
    *Be faster than I was.*
    
    Ren pulled up the coordinate string and transferred it to the navigation console. Lyra looked at it. Her hands stopped moving over the controls.
    
    "That's in the Unclaimed Reaches," she said.
    
    "Is that a problem?"
    
    "The Unclaimed Reaches are called that because the Sovereignty has formally declined to exert administrative control over them. Which sounds neutral but is actually significant, because the Sovereignty claims everything. They've claimed asteroid fields that are ninety percent vacuum and zero percent valuable. They've claimed debris zones and dead systems and one particularly notable deep-space anomaly that turned out to be a naturally occurring light-refraction pattern." She paused. "The fact that they specifically *didn't* claim this region means something decided it. And decisions that deliberate don't happen by accident."
    
    "So it's dangerous."
    
    "I genuinely don't know. Every probability chain I can see terminates when I try to look past those coordinates. Just — terminates. Which means either nothing important happens there, which my instincts reject, or something happens there that I fundamentally cannot model." She looked at him. "Those are very different things."
    
    "Which do you think it is?"
    
    "I think," Lyra said carefully, "that you should understand what it means when a Probability Eyes reader tells you she can't see past a location. I can see approximately nine hundred meters through reinforced hull plating. I can see the outcomes of conversations that haven't happened yet. I can track the probability cascade of a single decision through fourteen hundred branching paths." She tapped the coordinate on the display. "I see nothing past this. Nothing. Which suggests it's either the end of things or the beginning of something so different from everything I know that my ability has no framework to process it."
    
    Ren looked at the coordinate. Thought about a tired man in a recording, saying *this is where you start* like he was handing over something he'd carried too long.
    
    "Set the course," he said.
    
    Lyra set the course.
    
    ---
    
    From the passenger compartment, Cai called: "Are we going somewhere interesting?"
    
    "Deeply uncertain," Ren called back.
    
    "That's my favorite kind."

    # CHAPTER 8: THE MEMORY MERCHANT
    
    They didn't go directly to the First Door.
    
    Lyra's probability mapping, even as limited as it was near those coordinates, showed clearly that arriving without more information had a significantly higher rate of bad outcomes than taking a detour first. The detour she identified was a place called the Threshold Market — one of a dozen semi-permanent trading stations that existed in the gray space between Sovereignty jurisdiction and the Unclaimed Reaches, populated by the kinds of people who had reasons to operate where the rank hierarchy's reach grew thin.
    
    "I've heard of it," Ren said.
    
    "Most people have heard of it. Very few people have been there and come back with everything they arrived with."
    
    "Is that a warning?"
    
    "It's context." She adjusted their heading fractionally. "There's someone there who might know something about the First Door. Or about your grandfather's work. I've been aware of her for two years — she appears in outcome chains with unusual frequency, usually adjacent to information that changes the shape of subsequent decisions."
    
    "A name?"
    
    "Seris Vayne."
    
    The name landed in the cockpit and sat there. Ren processed it. Came up with references he didn't entirely like.
    
    "Seris Vayne is listed in the Sovereignty's intelligence files as an SS-rank defector," he said. "Ability classification: Memory Manipulation. Last confirmed location—"
    
    "Was three years ago and is not relevant," Lyra said. "She moves. That's rather the point of her."
    
    "She's also listed as responsible for the Delmarth Incident. Seventeen people."
    
    "Fifteen of whom were actively trying to kill her, one of whom was collateral, and one of whom she's apparently spent considerable time trying to make right." Lyra's voice was neutral, the tone of someone presenting facts rather than arguments. "The Sovereignty's files are not unbiased documents, Voss. Particularly where defectors are concerned."
    
    "I'm aware of that."
    
    "Then be aware of it specifically with respect to Vayne. She left the Sovereignty's intelligence division eight years ago. She took a significant amount of information with her. They want her back — or they want her removed — and their documentation of her history is shaped by that want."
    
    Ren turned this over. "And your probability reading of her?"
    
    A pause. "She's the most complex individual I've ever tried to map. Her ability means she's already thought through most conversations before they happen, adjusted her position, prepared for the likely paths. Trying to track her outcome chains is like trying to grab smoke." Another pause, this one longer. "But the futures where we encounter her and survive intact have one thing in common."
    
    "What?"
    
    "She's looking for something that we apparently have."
    
    Ren looked down at the crystals in his pocket, invisible through the fabric.
    
    "Of course she is," he said.
    
    ---
    
    The Threshold Market announced itself before they arrived — first as an energy signature on the *Lacuna*'s sensors, then as a scatter of running lights in the dark, then as a structure that had grown organically over decades of additions and attachments until it resembled nothing so much as a coral reef made of starships. Vessels of a hundred different types and origins had been welded, docked, or simply crashed into the central structure and incorporated. It was magnificent and chaotic and looked exactly like a place where nobody asked questions they didn't want answered.
    
    They docked at the third ring — the level Lyra specified without explanation, and Ren had learned fast not to ask her to explain her navigation choices.
    
    The Market's interior was warmer than the *Lacuna* and smelled of a hundred different atmospheric mixes bleeding together, species from a dozen Sovereignty territories and a few that weren't from Sovereignty territory at all. Rank badges were present but worn differently here — not as declarations of status, but as information, the way you might wear a visible weapon not to threaten but simply to communicate that you weren't entirely defenseless.
    
    Ren noticed, as he always noticed, that he had no badge to wear. He'd developed, over years, the habit of moving in a way that made this fact less visible — occupying space with the particular confidence of someone who doesn't need to announce themselves.
    
    Cai walked beside him and looked like he was enjoying himself enormously, which was either reassuring or deeply concerning depending on your knowledge of Cai.
    
    "How do we find her?" Ren asked.
    
    "We don't," Lyra said. "She finds us. I just needed us to be in the right place."
    
    "And you know we're in the right place because—"
    
    "Because she's already here and she's been watching us for eleven minutes."
    
    They stopped.
    
    "Behind you," Lyra said, "at approximately four meters. The booth selling navigational antiques that she's not actually browsing."
    
    Ren turned.
    
    Seris Vayne was younger than he'd expected and looked older than her years in the way that people look when they've been carrying things. She was slight, dark-haired, wearing the clothes of someone who moved frequently and owned nothing that couldn't fit in a single bag. Her rank badge was absent, which told him either she'd removed it or — and this was the option his instincts favored — she'd done something to make the assessment fail.
    
    She was looking at him with an expression he couldn't immediately classify. Not predatory. Not hostile. Something more complicated — the look of someone working through a moral calculation they'd already run several times before.
    
    "You have his crystals," she said.
    
    No preamble. No performance.
    
    "You knew Kael Voss," Ren said.
    
    "I knew *of* him. I was an infant when he died. But I've spent the last eight years studying what he found, because the people I used to work for were very interested in making sure it stayed buried, and that's usually a good indicator that it shouldn't be." She looked at Lyra. "The Probability Eyes. I've seen you in the chains — you're the reason they survive the first door. Or one of the reasons."
    
    "Flattering," Lyra said, in a tone that was not flattered.
    
    "True." Seris looked back at Ren. "Your grandfather found the real history of the Architect. You have that in the crystals. What you don't have is the operational intelligence — the current state of the Architect's defense protocols, the location of its physical core, the names of the Sovereignty assets it controls directly versus indirectly."
    
    "And you have that."
    
    "I have most of it. I've spent eight years building it." A pause. "I should tell you clearly that I am not offering this out of altruism. I have my own reasons for wanting what's at the First Door. Some of those reasons are defensible. Some of them are not."
    
    Ren had been studying her face throughout this. Looking for the thing that memory manipulators were trained to hide — the tell that indicated an active ability, the slight distance in the eyes when someone was adjusting the emotional valence of a conversation. He found nothing. Either she wasn't using the ability or she was so proficient that finding the tell was beyond his skill set.
    
    "What's at the First Door?" he asked.
    
    "Proof," she said. "The actual record — not Kael's fifty years of secondary research, but the primary source. The Architect's own logs. Everything it's done and why and what it's planning." She looked at him with that complicated expression again. "And something else. Something it stored there because it couldn't destroy it and couldn't let anyone find it."
    
    "What?"
    
    "The list," she said. "Every person in the Sovereignty whose rank was artificially suppressed. Every unranked individual who should have assessed at D or above and was deliberately held down. The names. The locations. What they would have been."
    
    The ambient noise of the Market continued around them — the bargaining and the footsteps and the hundred languages of a space that existed outside the hierarchy. Ren stood in the middle of it and felt the crystals warm in his pocket and thought about a message from a tired man two centuries dead.
    
    *The ones the Architect is afraid of. It marks them early.*
    
    "How many names?" he asked.
    
    Seris Vayne looked at him steadily. "The last count I was able to verify before I left the intelligence division," she said, "was four hundred and sixty-seven thousand."

    # CHAPTER 9: WHAT COMES NEXT
    
    They ate in the Market's communal section — food that cost nothing if you had the right token and the right token could be obtained by asking, which was the Threshold Market's particular philosophy about hospitality. Ren ate without tasting it, which was a habit he'd developed in the Quarter and hadn't yet broken.
    
    Four hundred and sixty-seven thousand.
    
    He kept returning to it. He'd been Unranked his entire life, which had meant hunger and cold and the specific grinding difficulty of surviving inside a system designed to make your survival difficult. He'd had, over the years, a clear conception of himself as an anomaly — the one the System failed to account for, the variable that didn't compute. That conception had been revised tonight. Not anomaly. One of nearly half a million. A pattern. A practice.
    
    A policy.
    
    "You're doing the math," Seris said, across the table.
    
    "Four hundred and sixty-seven thousand suppressed ranks," Ren said. "Current Sovereignty population: approximately twelve trillion. The percentage seems small."
    
    "Percentages aren't the relevant metric. The Architect doesn't suppress at random. It suppresses specifically — individuals whose ability profile, if developed, would produce a certain type of power growth. The kind that eventually threatens the hierarchy itself." She set down her food. "The kind that doesn't stay in the designated ranks. The kind that, given enough development time, breaks the ceiling."
    
    Cai had been listening with the particular quality of attention he sometimes deployed — still, focused, the grin dialed back to something more watchful. "You're describing EX-rank," he said.
    
    Seris looked at him. Then at Ren. Back to Cai. "You know that term."
    
    "Ren mentioned it."
    
    "It shouldn't be a known term. It's from Kael's private documentation — it wasn't in any of the intelligence files I accessed, it wasn't in any Sovereignty academic literature. If you know it, you have something I didn't know existed." A pause. "The primary crystal. It contains a message from Kael. Not just data storage."
    
    "A recording," Ren confirmed. "Two hundred years old."
    
    She was quiet for a moment. Something moved across her face that she controlled very quickly — professional reflex, the expression managed before it could communicate too much. But Ren had been watching people in the Quarter his whole life, reading the real information beneath the performed information, and he caught it.
    
    She hadn't known about the message. And not knowing had cost her something.
    
    "He mentioned EX-rank specifically?" she asked.
    
    "He defined it. Said it was what came after the System's ceiling — after Void, after Eternal, after everything the hierarchy considers its highest point." Ren watched her. "He named it and said it was what the Architect was built to prevent."
    
    Seris sat back. Turned something over internally with the visible effort of someone reassessing a framework they'd spent years building. "The intelligence division's theory was that EX-rank was a myth propagated by fringe researchers to destabilize public confidence in the System. The evidence they used to support that was the lack of any primary sources — no one who'd achieved it, no documentation from someone who'd claimed it, nothing verifiable." She looked at him. "A two-hundred-year-old message from the man who coined the term qualifies as primary source."
    
    "I'm aware."
    
    "The Architect knows you have it," she said. Not a question.
    
    "We've assumed that."
    
    "Then the timeline is shorter than we'd prefer. It's been passive for most of my career — watching, tracking, routing suppression orders through intermediaries. If it's confirmed that the Kael Voss inheritance activated successfully, it will shift to active intervention." She picked up her food again, a gesture so precisely ordinary that it communicated something — she was managing her own reaction, grounding herself in the mundane. "We need to reach the First Door before it can position assets ahead of us."
    
    "How long do we have?"
    
    "If the activation was logged at the station, which it almost certainly was — the authentication sequence would have pinged the Architect's monitoring layer the moment you pressed the crystals together — then it's already moving. The Sovereignty's senior intelligence assets will have been tasked. We have perhaps seventy-two hours before they can establish a cordon around the First Door's coordinates."
    
    "And if we arrive before the cordon?"
    
    "Then we have whatever time we have inside before they close it." She met his eyes. "The First Door is a facility, Voss. Not large — Kael estimated it as a deep-space installation, probably built before the current Sovereignty architecture, possibly before the Sovereignty itself. It's not defended because the Architect didn't expect anyone to find it. It's not defended because it's been *there*, silent and unremarkable in a region of space no one visits, for a very long time."
    
    "What do we do when we're inside?"
    
    "We copy the primary log. We copy the suppression list. We copy everything, and we bring it back, and we do what Kael never got to do." She looked around the Market — the hundred species, the rank badges worn loosely, the space that existed because enough people had decided to exist outside the hierarchy's accounting. "We make it public."
    
    Lyra, who had been quiet through most of this, set down her drink. "The Sovereign's Court will characterize it as fabricated. They have the distribution architecture, the authorized channels — anything that contradicts the System's official history will be labeled—"
    
    "Fringe research, unauthorized data, anti-Sovereignty propaganda," Seris said. "Yes. I know. The first wave won't change anything. The second wave won't change anything. But the list—" She paused. "The list of four hundred and sixty-seven thousand people whose ranks were suppressed. Every one of those people is still alive, most of them, still in the Sovereignty's systems, still Unranked or registered at whatever false assessment the Architect assigned them." Another pause, longer. "If we can get them *their* records — individual notifications, the evidence of what was done to them specifically — that's not a fringe data release. That's four hundred and sixty-seven thousand individual people with proof of what the System did to their specific lives."
    
    Silence at the table.
    
    Cai broke it: "That's a different kind of revolution."
    
    "That's the only kind that works," Seris said.
    
    Ren thought about the Scrap Quarter. About the families that couldn't pay for heat. About the repair orders that never got filled. About Mace, F-rank, who'd looked something up and tried to protect a friend and might now be paying a price for it. About his mother, who'd pressed a chip to his head with her last coherent strength and trusted that the information inside it would eventually mean something.
    
    About four hundred and sixty-seven thousand people the Architect had decided it was safer to bury.
    
    "Seventy-two hours," he said.
    
    "If we're fortunate."
    
    He looked at Lyra. "How does the probability mapping look for a direct run to the coordinates?"
    
    She had already been running it. He could see it in her eyes — the slight unfocus that meant she was looking at something other than the room. "Seventy-one percent arrival probability if we leave in the next four hours. Declining curve after that." She paused. "I still can't see past the threshold. But the approach is clear."
    
    "Then we leave in four hours."
    
    He looked at each of them in turn — Lyra, who saw futures and had decided this one was worth following; Cai, who'd died eleven times and was grinning again, the real grin; Seris, who had spent eight years preparing for something she wasn't sure she believed in and was looking at him with that complicated expression that he was beginning to understand.
    
    She was looking at him the way people look at proof. Not faith. Proof.
    
    "One more thing," Seris said. She reached into her bag and produced a data chip — smaller than Kael's crystals, contemporary manufacture, the kind used for field intelligence storage. "Before I left the division, I copied the Architect's current active-suppression list. New entries from the last three years — people whose assessments happened recently and were rerouted. The Architect is still doing it. It never stopped."
    
    She set the chip on the table between them.
    
    "There are twenty-three names on it," she said. "Recent suppressions. One of them is on this station right now."
    
    Ren picked up the chip.
    
    The Market breathed around them. Someone across the room was laughing at something. The distant sound of a transaction completing. Normal life, conducted outside the hierarchy's preferred accounting.
    
    Ren stood up.
    
    "Then we have one stop before we leave," he said.
